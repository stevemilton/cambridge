Chapter 01

Introduction

One kitchen. One menu. Two very different nutritional jobs to do.

Who this plan is for

This book was written for a specific household, and it makes no apology for that. At one end of the table sits an adult man — 86kg, 180cm — who trains with weights on Tuesday and Thursday mornings, plays tennis once or twice a week, and wants to lose roughly half a kilogram per week while holding on to every gram of muscle he has built. At the other end sits his fifteen-year-old daughter: growing, active, playing sport, and categorically not on a diet.

Most meal plans force a family like this apart — his sad chicken and broccoli in one container, her separate dinner on a different shelf. This plan does the opposite. Every breakfast, lunch and dinner in this book is one recipe, cooked once, eaten together. The only difference between the two plates is the size of the carbohydrate portion and, occasionally, an extra spoonful of the good stuff — olive oil, feta, yogurt — on her side.

The food itself is Mediterranean in spirit: lemon, garlic, olive oil, herbs, char and colour. It is high in protein because both of you need it — him to protect muscle in a calorie deficit, her to grow. It is built entirely from ingredients you can buy in any UK supermarket on a normal family budget. And it is designed to taste like food you would choose, not food you have been sentenced to.

Why it works for him

A sensible fat-loss phase for an 86kg man rests on four pillars, and every recipe in this book was engineered around them:

  • A moderate deficit. At around 2,000 kcal per day against an estimated maintenance of roughly 2,500–2,600 kcal (given two lifting sessions and regular tennis), you create a deficit of about 500 kcal per day — almost exactly the rate needed to lose ~0.5kg per week without the muscle loss, hunger and rebound that crash diets cause.
  • High protein, spread evenly. 150–170g of protein per day, delivered in four to five feedings of 25–50g, is the single most protective thing you can do for muscle while dieting. Every meal in this book carries a serious protein load; nothing relies on bars or shakes.
  • Carbohydrates placed where they earn their keep. Training mornings (Tuesday and Thursday) get carbohydrate-forward breakfasts and lunches; rest-day dinners lean a little greener. You will never feel flat under a barbell on this plan.
  • Food you actually want to eat. Adherence beats optimisation every time. A plan you enjoy on week six is worth ten perfect plans abandoned in week two.

Why it works for her

A fifteen-year-old girl in a growth phase who plays sport needs more energy than many adults — typically 2,200–2,600 kcal per day or beyond, depending on training load. She needs abundant carbohydrate for fuel, protein for growth, calcium and iron in particular, and — critically — a healthy, unanxious relationship with food.

So her version of this plan follows three rules, stated once here and repeated throughout the book:

  • She never counts calories. No numbers, no tracking, no talk of deficits at the table. The macro panels in this book are for the adult portion only.
  • She eats the same food, with more carbohydrate. Every recipe includes a “For your daughter” note with a specific, generous adjustment — an extra 75–100g of cooked rice, a second pitta, a bigger portion of potatoes, full-fat yogurt instead of 0% — plus free access to bread, fruit, milk and snacks between meals.
  • She eats to appetite. The portions given for her are starting points, not ceilings. If she is hungrier — especially around matches, training or growth spurts — she eats more, without comment.

A note worth reading twice

Teenagers absorb attitudes about food faster than nutrients. The most valuable thing this plan can model for her is a parent who eats colourful, delicious, unrestricted-looking food with genuine pleasure. If any signs of food anxiety or restriction appear in a teenager, put the plan aside and speak to your GP.

How the eight weeks are structured

The engine of this book is a four-week rotation, run twice. Each of the four menu weeks uses all seven breakfasts, five or six of the twelve lunches and six or seven of the twelve dinners, arranged so that:

  • Batch-friendly recipes appear twice in a week (cook once, eat twice — this is a feature, not a shortcut);
  • Freezer-friendly dinners cooked in double quantity in weeks 1 and 2 reappear as near-zero-effort meals in weeks 3 and 4;
  • Training days (Tuesday and Thursday) always carry carbohydrate-forward meals before and after your 9–10am session;
  • Weekends get the slower, more social cooking — shakshuka, pancakes, steak night, fish pie.

Weeks 5–8 repeat weeks 1–4. By then the recipes will be familiar, the shopping automatic, and the prep session half the length. That repetition is deliberate: it is how a plan becomes a habit, and a habit becomes just the way your family eats.

What you will not find here

  • No protein bars, meal-replacement shakes or powders — every gram of protein comes from food.
  • No fad mechanics — no fasting windows, no food groups demonised, no “syns”.
  • No tiny portions. If a plate looks mean, the plan fails. These plates do not look mean.
  • No ingredient you cannot find in Tesco, Sainsbury’s, Asda, Morrisons, Aldi or Lidl.
  • No ultra-processed shortcuts — the most processed things in this book are wholemeal pitta, canned tomatoes, canned beans and feta.

Expectations, honestly set

At ~0.5kg per week, eight weeks should deliver roughly 3.5–4.5kg of fat loss — but the scale will not move in a straight line. Expect a fast first week (mostly water and glycogen), flat weeks that mysteriously follow big tennis weekends, and whooshes after quiet ones. Weigh yourself two or three mornings a week, average the numbers, and judge the trend over fortnights, not days. If the fortnightly average is falling by around 0.4–0.7kg per week, the plan is working — keep going. If it stalls for two full weeks, trim 100–150 kcal from the day (the easiest place is the evening snack) rather than slashing meals.

Enough talk. Chapter 2 sets the numbers, then we cook.
